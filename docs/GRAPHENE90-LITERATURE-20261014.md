The earlier graphene refinement tested synthetic behavior and interior measurements; its own handoff says real graphene accuracy remains unvalidated (`research/graphene-refinement-20261009/HANDOFF.txt:5–24`). A successful software test is therefore not evidence of segmentation precision or recall on this scan.

Blake et al. show that graphene visibility depends strongly on oxide thickness and wavelength. Their paper identifies approximately 90 and 280 nm oxide as favorable for green/white-light viewing and favors the lower thickness for visibility. This does not mean that 90 nm and 285 nm give interchangeable RGB values. Their model uses a constant graphite-like refractive index and notes finite-NA deviations and nominal oxide variation; the actual fitted thin-oxide example was 88 nm. This supports the repository's conditional optics approach, but not a universal percentage threshold. [Primary paper, *Making graphene visible*, Applied Physics Letters 91, 063124 (2007)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0705.0259), [published DOI](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.2768624).

Uslu et al.'s 2DMatGMM is particularly relevant: its graphene domain is nominal 90 nm SiO₂ and 20× imaging, using a Nikon Eclipse and Imaging Source DFK 33UX174 camera. It combines substrate-normalized contrast populations with a shape classifier. Red/green contrast is less sensitive than blue to oxide variation near 90 nm; camera spectral response and illumination still enter the contrast integral. Its normalization includes dark and flat-field correction. These findings support examining local red/green populations and contamination examples, rather than treating the imported RGB means as calibrated for our ZEISS/Axiocam images. Their contrast convention is opposite the repository convention. [Primary paper, sections I–IV, *An open-source robust machine learning platform for real-time detection and classification of 2D material flakes* (2024)](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.14845v3), [published DOI](https://doi.org/10.1088/2632-2153/ad2287).

The authors' released dataset contains graphene classes 1–4, with 425 training and 1,362 test images. Provided semantic masks omit instances below 200 pixels, and their COCO evaluation uses the `_300` instance subset. Consequently, those released metrics do not establish performance on arbitrarily tiny flakes at our pixel sampling. [Primary dataset documentation](https://github.com/Jaluus/2DMatGMM/blob/main/Datasets/README.md).

MaskTerial is an optional later comparator, not a presently validated solution for this run. Its paper reports synthetic pretraining and adaptation with 5–10 images; that is not evidence that arbitrary pretrained weights produce accurate local graphene masks without adaptation and evaluation. The repository's offline-analysis notes explicitly do not claim a locally fine-tuned model. [Primary MaskTerial paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09333), [published DOI](https://doi.org/10.1039/D5DD00156K); local context: `docs/OFFLINE-ANALYSIS.txt:133–145`.

Preserve original files and record any reconstructed metadata or label evidence explicitly. Use the separately audited recovery input route rather than bypass hashes or silently skip those fields.
2. Produce high-recall masks and a review set spanning substrate, obvious flakes, thin-looking regions, thick crystals, residue, boundaries and different field locations. Keep unmatched regions visible as unknown. Compare the conditional graphene90 model with the external population prior and observed local red/green distributions; do not tune by looking only at already-selected positives.
3. Fit local empirical populations only from reviewed examples, then evaluate on held-out fields or spatial groups. Report instance recall/precision, missed small regions, false positives and mask quality by size; a high background-dominated pixel accuracy alone is not useful. Existing default size/shape/contrast gates should remain exposed in the report rather than masquerade as physical cutoffs.
4. Export source-coordinate masks, polygons, areas, interior contrast and uncertainty, and review crops. Mark detections as graphene candidates or optical populations. Keep layer number unknown unless independently supported by local labeled references or subsequent characterization. Stage coordinates remain historical observations after manual takeover, not motion authorization.

## Operator bands and extrapolation — October 14 addendum

The saved `research/graphene-segmentation-20261014/operator-contrast-observations-20261014.json` supplies three operator-associated groups around red contrast 0.12, 0.26 and 0.35. Their median normalized RGB directions are respectively `(1, 0.7626, 0.4756)`, `(1, 0.7742, 0.4787)` and `(1, 0.7707, 0.4981)`. These support a shared local color trajectory as a working hypothesis. Exact point identities remain inferred, and repeated fields can show the same physical flake. These are not independent layer standards. Unequal separations between the three amplitudes also do not establish a common layer increment.

The physical basis for cautious extension is real. Abergel, Russell and Fal'ko predict approximately twice the optical visibility for bilayer versus monolayer graphene in their visible-light model. This supports a first-order thin-material approximation; it does not establish linearity at arbitrary thickness or identify our bands. [Primary paper, *Visibility of graphene flakes on a dielectric substrate* (2007)](https://arxiv.org/abs/0705.0091), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.2768625).

For broader thickness ranges, the multilayer reflection equations retain thickness-dependent optical phase and multiple reflections. A local linear approximation is therefore not a universal straight RGB ray. Near 90 nm and near 285 nm oxide, the different interference conditions produce different trajectories. [Blake et al., reflection model](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0705.0259). Jessen et al. calculate RGB values by integrating the reflected spectrum against the light source and detector channel responses; their method includes graphene on 90 nm oxide. This explains why normalizing to nearby substrate does not eliminate dependence on spectral illumination and camera response. [Primary paper, *Quantitative optical mapping of two-dimensional materials* (2018)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5913130/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-23922-1).

Li et al. demonstrated empirical optical identification through 15 layers on 90/300 nm oxide using measured contrast tables. That supports collecting local standards over the desired thickness range, rather than extending three unlabeled populations indefinitely. Their contrast-difference convention must not be copied numerically into our normalized contrast. [Primary paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1310.6863), [publisher supporting-data collection](https://acs.figshare.com/collections/Rapid_and_Reliable_Thickness_Identification_of_Two_Dimensional_Nanosheets_Using_Optical_Microscopy/2375239).

Recommended implementation for the current re-segmentation (engineering proposal, not a validated classifier):

1. Keep the three observed empirical populations and an explicitly local achromatic negative population. Add a continuous trajectory proposal, approximately `c = b + a*d`, where `d = (1, 0.77, 0.48)`. Estimate `b` from substrate residuals; do not obtain an unconstrained intercept and layer increment from three unlabeled groups. Keep `a` continuous and layer count unknown.
2. Score distance perpendicular to the trajectory using measured residual covariance plus image measurement noise. For covariance `S`, the projected amplitude is `a = (dᵀ S⁻¹ (c-b))/(dᵀ S⁻¹ d)`. Longitudinal spread should not widen the classifier toward achromatic `(1,1,1)` contamination. Near zero contrast, compare against substrate noise; channel ratios become unstable. Any Gaussian implementation of the corridor should carry separate proposal provenance rather than inventing observed populations.
3. Interpolate within the observed amplitude range; flag candidates beyond it as extrapolated. If a bounded pilot interval is needed, extending by one largest adjacent observed band-center gap would reach roughly red contrast 0.50. This is an explicit review heuristic, **not** a physical cutoff or proof of valid graphene contrast. Preserve farther and off-trajectory objects through the generic unknown/gate path. Do not automatically convert extrapolated candidates into calibrated material labels.
4. Fit and validate with eroded interiors and held-out fields or physical-flake groups. Inspect thin-looking positives and achromatic negatives, including the reported points, rather than fitting to every preselected pixel. Optical agreement alone does not establish chemical identity. A mixed edge pixel can fall between real terrace populations, so a trajectory match alone is not a new thickness population.
5. For wider extrapolation, evaluate the existing spectral transfer-matrix model across thickness, oxide and optical-context uncertainty, and test whether its local tangent agrees with these observations. Three unlabeled amplitudes cannot identify oxide thickness, lamp spectrum, camera response and absolute layer numbers simultaneously. Use independently characterized local graphene standards before reporting absolute layers; retain uncertain latent thickness and separate model-supported proposals until then.

The empirical ray/corridor and optional interval above are our inference from this run's observations and the optical model, not thresholds prescribed by these papers. No segmentation outputs or raw scientific data were changed by this literature addendum.
